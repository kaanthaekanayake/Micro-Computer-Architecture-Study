\section{Introduction}
The fast growth of intelligence, powerful computing and smart electronic systems is leading to new needs for 
computer designs and chip technology. Old ways of building computers are getting better with ideas that make 
processing faster use less power, access memory better and allow more changes in hardware. This report looks 
at five new technologies that are shaping how modern small computers and electronic systems are made. Neural 
Processing Units and Neural Engines offer parts to make artificial intelligence and machine-learning tasks go 
faster. Unified CPU, GPU and RAM setups help parts of a computer share data and talk to each other better. 
RISC-V gives an adjustable way to build instruction sets with support for special operations. 
Three-dimensional mixed integration and Through-Silicon Vias offer ways to connect chips, memory and 
other parts in a tight space. Finally materials that are not based on silicon, like gallium nitride,
 silicon carbide and flat materials are being studied to solve the limits of silicon technology.

\section{Conclution}
The technologies discussed in this report show how microcomputer and semiconductor architectures are changing to 
meet the growing needs of computing systems. NPUs and Neural Engines give speed for artificial intelligence 
tasks while unified CPU, GPU and RAM designs cut data transfer time and help processing units work together
 better. RISC‑V gives an open and flexible instruction set that lets users tailor it and use vector processing 
 for different uses. Meanwhile 3D heterogeneous integration and TSV technology let processors, memory and other 
 parts be combined with tighter connections although heat control and making the chips still remain big problems. 
 Beyond‑silicon materials, like GaN, SiC and two‑dimensional materials give chances to improve power use, 
 high‑frequency operation and keep scaling devices. Together these developments show that future computing 
 systems will rely more on processors, smart memory designs, open hardware, advanced packaging and new 
 semiconductor materials instead of just making chips smaller.
